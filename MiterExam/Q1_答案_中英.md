# Q1. Evaluating a home-based fall-prevention programme fairly

## English

### (a)

People who exercised and people who did not exercise are different. A simple comparison of their fall rates can give the wrong answer.

Frailty changes over time. When an older adult becomes weaker, two things happen. Exercise becomes harder. A fall also becomes more likely. Past exercise can change later frailty. Later frailty then affects two things: whether the person keeps exercising, and whether a fall happens. Adherence works in the same way. This is called time-varying confounding.

So the exercise group keeps more of the healthier people. The no-exercise group keeps more of the frailer people. A lower fall rate in the exercise group may only show this health difference. It may not show that the programme itself prevents falls.

A large sample does not fix this problem. It only makes the same unfair comparison look more exact.

### (b)

Time zero is the day we start to follow each person. On that day, three things happen together. The person meets the entry rules. We set the strategy: start the home exercise programme, or do not start it. We also start the 90-day clock for falls. Both groups use the same day.

In this study, time zero is the baseline visit. On that day, the older adult lives in the community and can still choose either strategy. We then count falls for 90 days after that visit.

A wrong time zero creates immortal time bias. Suppose we call a person "treated" only after exercise has started. That person must have no fall before that day. If we count those fall-free days as time on the programme, we give exercise credit for days with no exercise yet. The programme looks helpful for the wrong reason.

Here is an example. For people who never exercise, we start the clock on the day they join the study. For people who exercise, we start the clock on their first exercise day. Falls before the first exercise day all go to the no-exercise group. Those early days add no falls to the exercise group. Exercise looks safer only because those early days were left out.

## 中文

### （a）

做了运动的人和没做运动的人，本来就不是同一类人。直接比较他们的跌倒率，很容易得出错误结论。

衰弱会随时间变化。老年人身体变差时，会发生两件事。运动更难坚持下去。跌倒也更容易发生。以前的运动还会改变后来的衰弱程度。后来的衰弱又会影响两件事：还会不会继续运动，以及会不会跌倒。依从性也是这样。这叫做时变混杂。

因此，运动组里留下的，更多是身体还好的人。不运动组里留下的，更多是身体变差的人。运动组跌倒较少，可能只是因为他们身体更好，不一定是运动方案真的预防了跌倒。

样本很大也解决不了这个问题。它只是让这个不公平的比较看起来更精确。

### （b）

时间零点就是我们开始随访每一个人的那一天。这一天要同时做三件事。确认这个人符合纳入条件。确定策略：开始居家运动，或者不开始。同时开始计算 90 天内的跌倒。两组要用同一天。

在这项研究里，时间零点就是基线评估那一天。这一天，老年人住在社区，两种策略都还可以选择。然后我们统计这一天之后 90 天内的跌倒。

时间零点选错，就会产生不死时间偏倚。假如一个人要等运动开始后，才被算作“接受了治疗”，那么在这之前他必须没有跌倒。如果我们把这些没有跌倒的日子算进运动方案，就等于让运动为“还没开始运动的日子”邀功。方案看起来有效，原因是错的。

举一个例子。从不运动的人，从入组那天开始计时。做运动的人，从第一次运动那天开始计时。第一次运动之前的跌倒，全部算到不运动组。这些早期的日子也不会给运动组增加跌倒。运动看起来更安全，只是因为这些早期日子被漏掉了。
